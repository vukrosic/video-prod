# How Long Would You Survive in Every Era of Earth's History?

**Format:** animated explainer (Kurzgesagt-style)
**Target length:** ~13–14 min (~2,000 words of narration at ~150 wpm)
**Series:** Episode 1 of "How Long Would You Survive..." (sequels: every planet, every ocean depth, every century)

---

## Packaging

### Title options
1. **How Long Would You Survive in Every Era of Earth's History?** (clear, searchable; default)
2. You vs. 4.5 Billion Years of Earth
3. You Could Only Survive on Earth for the Last 10% of Its History
4. I Dropped You Into Every Era of Earth. You Died Fast.

### Thumbnail concepts
- **A (default):** the character in a t-shirt standing on glowing magma, with a huge red timer reading **"0:01"**
  in the corner. No other text.
- **B:** a vertical split with three panels (lava / giant dragonfly / T. rex), each with its own timer:
  "1 SEC", "3 YEARS", "?"
- **C:** the character looking up at a gigantic Moon filling half the sky, with the timer "2 MIN"

### Recurring visual system (reused in every episode of the series)
- **The character:** a simple human in a t-shirt and jeans. The same character appears in every episode.
- **Survival timer** (top right): counts up while the character is alive and flashes red at death.
- **Deep-time clock** (bottom bar): "X years ago" on a 4.5-billion-year track.
- **Death counter** (optional running gag): a small skull tally in the corner.

## Script

> **Legend:** **[V]** = visual direction · **[T]** = timer state · **[SFX]** = sound

---

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:35)

**[V]** Black screen. A low rumble. Fade in: an orange glow. The character materializes, standing on a
cooling crust of rock over a sea of magma. The sky is a haze of glowing vapor.
**[T]** 0:00 → starts counting.

> You wake up. It's hot. Very hot.
>
> It's 4.5 billion years ago. Earth has just been hit by a planet the size of Mars. The impact melted
> the surface into an ocean of magma, and the air is so hot it contains vaporized rock.
>
> You take one breath.

**[T]** Stops at **~0:01**. Flashes red.
**[SFX]** A sharp hiss, then silence.

> That was your whole visit.
>
> Let's try again. A little later this time.

**[V]** Title card: **HOW LONG WOULD YOU SURVIVE IN EVERY ERA OF EARTH'S HISTORY?**

---

### THE RULES (0:35–1:05)

**[V]** The character on a plain background. Rules pop up one by one as icons.

> Here are the rules. You show up in normal clothes, with no tools, no food and no spacesuit.
> You arrive on land, or on the closest thing to land that exists. We start the clock when you arrive
> and stop it when you die.
>
> Earth has existed for 4.5 billion years. You'd think a planet where humans evolved would be a pretty
> good place for humans for most of that time.
>
> You would be wrong.

---

### STOP 1 — THE HADEAN, 4 BILLION YEARS AGO (1:05–2:15)

**[V]** A dark, steaming world. Black volcanic islands in a hot, greenish ocean. In the sky: an
**enormous Moon**, several times larger than today's.
**[T]** Resets, starts counting.

> Half a billion years later, the magma has cooled. There's solid ground, and there are oceans.
> There's water.
>
> And look up: the Moon. It formed much closer to Earth and has been drifting away ever since.
> Right now it looks several times bigger than the Moon you know. Its pull is so strong that the tides
> are enormous, and Earth spins so fast that a day lasts only a fraction of 24 hours.
>
> It's beautiful. Enjoy it while you hold your breath, because there is no oxygen here. The air is
> mostly carbon dioxide and nitrogen, and it's thick and heavy.
>
> You can hold your breath for maybe a minute. Then your body forces you to breathe. When you breathe
> in air with no oxygen, it doesn't just fail to help you. It pulls oxygen *out* of your blood.
> You're unconscious within seconds.

**[T]** Stops at **~2 minutes**.

> Two minutes. That's a big improvement!
>
> It's also as good as it's going to get for a very, very long time.

---

### STOP 2 — THE ARCHEAN, 3.5 BILLION YEARS AGO (2:15–3:15)

**[V]** A shallow, calm sea. Along the shore: strange rounded rock domes, **stromatolites**, built
by mats of microbes. The Sun looks slightly smaller and dimmer.
**[T]** Resets, counting.

> Something has changed. There is life.
>
> These domes are cities of microbes, some of the first living things on Earth. You're looking at
> your extremely distant relatives.
>
> The Sun is weaker, only about three-quarters as bright as today. Scientists still argue about why
> Earth wasn't frozen solid. The best guess is that huge amounts of greenhouse gases kept it warm.
> The sky may even have had an orange haze from methane.
>
> The one thing that hasn't changed is the air. There's still no oxygen.

**[T]** Stops at **~2 minutes**.

> Life is here, and it can't help you.

---

### STOP 3 — THE GREAT OXIDATION, 2.4 BILLION YEARS AGO (3:15–4:30)

**[V]** Blue-green microbes (cyanobacteria) in the ocean, each releasing tiny bubbles. Rust-red bands
spread through the seafloor rock. The sky subtly shifts.
**[T]** Resets, counting.

> Then some microbes invented something new: photosynthesis that uses sunlight and water, and releases
> oxygen as waste.
>
> For hundreds of millions of years, that oxygen got absorbed by iron in the oceans, which is why
> rocks from this time have bright red stripes of rust. Eventually the iron ran out, and oxygen started
> building up in the air.
>
> This was a disaster for most life on Earth. To the microbes that ruled the planet, oxygen was poison.
> It may be the first time life polluted its own planet.
>
> For you, it's great news. There's oxygen!

**[V]** The character inhales hopefully. A gauge appears: "O₂ — today: 21%". The needle barely moves:
**"~0.2–2%"**.

> A tiny amount of oxygen. Probably somewhere around 1 to 10 percent of today's level.
> Even on the summit of Mount Everest, you get roughly the equivalent of 7% oxygen, and most people
> need extra oxygen to survive there.
>
> This is far below that.

**[T]** Stops at **~2–3 minutes**.

> A few more seconds, maybe. That's all.

---

### STOP 4 — THE BORING BILLION (4:30–5:30)

**[V]** A fast montage. The deep-time clock races from 1.8 billion to 0.8 billion years ago.
The character appears in short flashes, and each time the timer stops at ~2–3 minutes.
The skull tally goes up: ✕ ✕ ✕ ✕ ✕.

> What comes next is a period geologists call, quite literally, the "Boring Billion."
>
> For about a billion years, almost nothing happens. Oxygen stays low. Life stays mostly microscopic.
> The biggest change is that days slowly get longer as the Moon drifts away. 1.4 billion years ago,
> a day lasted about 18 hours.
>
> Let's step back and look at the whole picture.

**[V]** Zoom out to the full deep-time bar. From 4.5 billion years ago to about 600 million
years ago, the bar is almost entirely red.

> Out of Earth's 4.5 billion years, you'd suffocate within minutes for about **four billion** of them.
> For almost 90% of Earth's history, this planet would kill you before you finished reading this sentence.
>
> But things are about to get dramatic.

---

### STOP 5 — SNOWBALL EARTH, 650 MILLION YEARS AGO (5:30–6:30)

**[V]** A white planet seen from space. Then the surface: endless ice, even at the equator,
under a pale Sun. Wind noise.
**[T]** Resets, counting.

> 650 million years ago, Earth froze.
>
> Ice sheets may have reached all the way to the equator. From space the planet would look like
> a giant snowball, and that's exactly what scientists call it: Snowball Earth.
>
> Oxygen was rising around this time, but probably not enough yet. You'd still suffocate in minutes.
>
> And even if someone handed you an oxygen tank, your t-shirt would lose to the cold. With temperatures
> far below freezing and no shelter, you'd freeze to death within hours.

**[T]** Stops at **~3 minutes**. A small note appears: *"with oxygen tank: a few hours"*.

> Still, something important happened in this frozen world. When the ice finally melted, life
> exploded.

---

### STOP 6 — THE CAMBRIAN, 520 MILLION YEARS AGO (6:30–8:00)

**[V]** A bare, rocky coastline: no plants, no soil, just stone and wind. The character stands at
the waterline. The camera dips underwater: trilobites, jellyfish, and the large predator **Anomalocaris**.
**[T]** Resets, counting... 1 min... 2 min... 3 min...

> 520 million years ago. You arrive, take a breath, and wait.
>
> Three minutes. Four. Five.

**[T]** Keeps counting past every earlier record. **[SFX]** Music swells.

> You're breathing.
>
> The air is probably still thinner than today's. It might feel like standing on a high mountain:
> dizzy, short of breath, with a headache. Scientists are still debating exactly how much oxygen there
> was. But for the first time in this video, you're alive.
>
> So now you have new problems.
>
> Look around. There are no plants on land. No grass, no trees, no soil, no insects. The continents are
> bare rock. There is no wood, so there's no fire. There's no shelter except the rocks.
>
> All life is in the sea, and it's having a party. This is the Cambrian Explosion, when most of the
> major animal groups that exist today appear in the fossil record in a geologic eyeblink. Trilobites
> crawl across the seafloor, and swimming above them is Anomalocaris, one of the first big predators.
>
> Your only food is in the water. You'd have to catch shellfish and trilobites with your bare hands
> and eat them raw, with no idea whether they're poisonous.
>
> With a lot of luck and a lot of trilobites...

**[T]** Stops at **~a few weeks**. The timer display switches units from minutes to days.

> ...you might last a few weeks. Your first real survival story.

---

### STOP 7 — THE CARBONIFEROUS, 300 MILLION YEARS AGO (8:00–9:30)

**[V]** A dense, humid swamp forest of giant club-moss trees. A dragonfly the size of a seagull
(**Meganeura**) zooms past the character's head. A millipede longer than a person
(**Arthropleura**) crawls over a log.
**[T]** Resets, counting.

> 300 million years ago, plants have conquered the land. Enormous swamp forests cover the continents.
>
> Take a deep breath. The air might be around **30–35% oxygen**, far more than today's 21%.
> You feel great.
>
> The insects feel great too. Oxygen levels this high may be part of why insects could grow so large.
> That's a dragonfly with a wingspan of about 70 centimeters. And that's a millipede called
> Arthropleura, up to 2.5 meters long. It's thought to have been a plant-eater, which is lucky for you.
>
> There's food: amphibians, fish, early reptiles. There's firewood everywhere. You could build a shelter.
> There aren't even any germs that evolved to infect humans. The diseases that plague us today won't
> exist for hundreds of millions of years.
>
> But all that oxygen has a dark side: fire. With this much oxygen, even damp plants can burn.
> A single lightning strike can set off a wildfire that races through the forest.

**[V]** Lightning. The forest catches fire. The character runs toward a river.

> If you stay near water and keep your fire under control, you could honestly live here a long time.

**[T]** Stops at **~years**. The display changes to years.

> This might be the best era so far. Let's see if the good times last.

---

### STOP 8 — THE GREAT DYING, 252 MILLION YEARS AGO (9:30–10:45)

**[V]** A scorched, brown landscape. The sky is thick with haze. In the distance, glowing lava flows
spread across the horizon (the **Siberian Traps**). Dead trees.
**[T]** Resets, counting.

> They don't.
>
> 252 million years ago, in what is now Siberia, volcanic eruptions poured out enough lava to cover
> an area the size of a continent, over hundreds of thousands of years. They released gigantic amounts
> of carbon dioxide.
>
> The planet overheated. The oceans lost their oxygen. Near the equator, sea temperatures may have
> reached around 40°C, the temperature of a hot bath. On land, it may have been even hotter.
>
> This is the Permian–Triassic extinction, also called the Great Dying. Around 90% of all marine
> species and most land animals disappeared. It's the worst mass extinction in Earth's history.

**[T]** Stops at **~hours** (at the equator).

> Arrive near the equator, and the heat could kill you within hours.
>
> Earth would recover, but it took millions of years. And what came out the other side was a new
> world, ruled by reptiles.

---

### STOP 9 — THE LATE CRETACEOUS, 66 MILLION YEARS AGO (10:45–12:15)

**[V]** A green, warm landscape with flowering plants. Herds of Triceratops. The character has built
a small hut near a river. A calendar scratched on a rock shows many tally marks.
**[T]** Starts counting. The display moves quickly to **~1 year**.

> 66 million years ago. The age of dinosaurs.
>
> This time let's say you're lucky. You arrive somewhere quiet. You've learned to fish. You've learned
> which plants make you sick. You've learned that you are, basically, a snack-sized mammal, and that
> the smart move is to avoid being seen by anything with big teeth.
>
> You've survived for a whole year. This is by far your best run.
>
> One afternoon, a new star appears in the sky.

**[V]** A bright point of light, growing. **[SFX]** Everything goes quiet.

> It's an asteroid about 10 kilometers wide, heading for what is now Mexico.
>
> Where it hits, everything is vaporized instantly. Across the planet, debris thrown into space falls
> back through the atmosphere. Some scientists think the sky may have glowed hot enough to start
> fires across entire continents. Then comes the dark: soot and dust block out the Sun for months or
> even years. Plants die. Then the animals that eat plants. Then the animals that eat *them*.
>
> About three-quarters of all species on Earth die out.

**[T]** Stops at **~1 year + a few weeks**.

> If you survive the first day, the dark winter will probably finish you off.
>
> But in the ashes, some small, furry animals made it through: our ancestors.

---

### STOP 10 — THE ICE AGE, 20,000 YEARS AGO (12:15–13:15)

**[V]** A cold grassland. Mammoths in the distance. Smoke rising from a camp. Figures in fur coats.
They turn to look at the character.
**[T]** Resets, counting.

> 20,000 years ago. The last Ice Age is at its peak. So much water is locked in ice that sea levels
> are about 120 meters lower than today.
>
> And for the first time, you're not alone. Those are humans, exactly like you.
>
> If they welcome you, you could actually live here, possibly for the rest of your life. You'd be
> clumsy at first. You'd need to learn to hunt, make tools and build fires. But this is the world
> your body was built for.
>
> There's just one twist. You might be more dangerous to *them* than they are to you.
>
> Many of the diseases we carry today are young. Measles, for example, probably only appeared a few
> thousand years ago, after people started living close to farm animals. Your immune system has seen
> things theirs never has.

**[T]** Keeps counting... **decades**.

> Maybe a lifetime. If you're careful, and they're lucky.

---

### STOP 11 — TODAY (13:15–14:00)

**[V]** The character sitting on a couch at home, phone in hand. The timer ticks peacefully.
**[T]** Shows **~73 years** (roughly the global average lifespan).

> And now you're here. Today, the timer shows a whole lifetime.

**[V]** Zoom out to the full deep-time bar compressed into a 24-hour clock.

> Squeeze Earth's entire history into a single day. For the first 21 hours or so, you couldn't
> breathe. You could only survive without protection in roughly the last two or three hours. Humans
> like us have existed for about the last five seconds.
>
> And the window isn't open forever. The Sun is slowly getting brighter. In roughly a billion years,
> it will be too hot for oceans, and the timer will start shrinking again, back toward one second.

**[V]** A slow push in on the character, looking up at the sky. Quiet music.

> We didn't just get lucky to be alive. We showed up during the short window when this planet lets
> us live on it with nothing but a t-shirt.
>
> Enjoy the air. It took four billion years to make.

**[V]** End card: *"Next time: How long would you survive on every planet?"*

## Production notes
- **Pacing:** the first 5 minutes (4 billion years of dying) are the retention risk. Keep those stops
  short and funny, and use the "Boring Billion" montage and the "90% red bar" reveal as the payoff.
- **The Cambrian moment is the emotional turn.** Let the timer run past the old record with the music
  swelling. That's the clip for Shorts.
- **Shorts cut-downs:** (1) Cold open "one breath" (~30 s), (2) Cambrian timer moment, (3) the 24-hour
  clock ending, (4) the Ice Age measles twist.
- **Reusable assets for the series:** the character, the timer UI, the deep-time bar, the skull tally,
  and the end card template.
